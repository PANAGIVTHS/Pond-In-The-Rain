This Pond

The background of this site is a small, living koi pond drawn entirely out of text characters. Koi swim, rain falls, lanterns drift and autumn leaves fall, and the pond reacts to you and to the page on top of it. There are no images, sprites, libraries or sound files: everything is computed in about 660 lines of plain JavaScript and painted onto one canvas, sixty times a second.

JavaScriptCanvas 2DWeb Audiono libraries~660 lines60 fps

How it's drawn

The screen is treated as a grid of character cells, like a terminal. Every frame the pond is built into a few layers, one value per cell: the water's light, ripple rings, lantern glow, the koi, the leaves and the title. Then each cell gets one character and one colour. Shapes are never stored as pictures; they're described by maths and "sampled" into cells, so the whole scene can move and resize freely.

  pond state            per-cell layers            one canvas
  koi, ripples,   ──►   glyph + colour      ──►   one character
  light, leaves         for every cell            per cell

The shimmering light on the water comes from two slowly drifting noise fields: where their values meet, light gathers into a moving net of · - ~. Ripples are rings drawn with whichever of - \ | / follows the ring's curve at that point, which is why they look round even though they're made of text.

The koi

Each koi is a chain of eleven points: the head swims, and the body follows the path the head took, so it bends naturally through turns. For every cell near a koi, the code finds the closest point on that spine, works out how far across the body the cell is, and shades it as if lit from the upper left, choosing a denser character for shadow and a lighter one for light (@%#&8o*+:). Nine real koi varieties (kohaku, tancho, showa, asagi and others) are just colour rules over the body, and eyes, a forked tail and pectoral fins are added on top.

Their behaviour is a few simple rules working together:

  • each koi has its own home patch and meanders around it, mostly forward, so the pond fills evenly
  • a koi can't turn tighter than its body can follow, so it never folds into itself
  • they keep their distance from each other's bodies, and now and then two swim side by side for a while
  • they flee from clicks, race for food, look at a lantern you light up, and keep out from under the name at the top
  • a koi will occasionally nudge a lantern that drifted behind the page back out to the side

Weather, leaves and sound

Rain drops make small ripples and streaks; lightning flickers the whole scene and the card borders, followed by thunder a moment later. It's always autumn: long olive leaves land, drift to the sides, then darken and sink. All the sound is synthesised live in the browser: rain is filtered pink noise, the drips and the lantern bell are tuned oscillators, and thunder is a burst of noise shaped into a crack and a long roll.

The pond and the page

The pond isn't just behind the page; the two talk to each other. Scrolling drifts the pond and stirs the water; each section has its own light and rain; a koi swimming under a card tints its border in that koi's colour; lanterns warm the cards they float near, raindrops slide down the glass, and the lanterns double as a map of the site.

What was hard

Making koi move like fish. Early versions folded into themselves, slid sideways in rigid U-shapes, or circled forever around a point they could never reach. Each fix came from finding the real cause rather than adding another rule: letting the body follow the head's path, and limiting how tightly a koi can turn based on its own length.

Stopping them from piling up. Watching wasn't enough to tell whether a change helped, so I built a test that fast-forwards fifteen minutes of pond time and counts overlaps and clumps. It showed that clever avoidance rules barely mattered; what mattered was how much open water there is, where the koi start, and keeping their home patches out from under the title.

A readable name made of text. Thin, elegant letters fall apart when they're turned into character cells. The name is now pre-rendered once and sampled into a classic ASCII density ramp, so it looks the same on every device.
